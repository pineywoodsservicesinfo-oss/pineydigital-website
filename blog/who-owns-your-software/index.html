Ownership

Who Owns Your Software? 8 Questions to Ask Any Developer Before You Sign

November 14, 2026 • 6 min read

You pay a developer to build something custom. It has your logo, your workflow, your data. So it's yours, right?

Not necessarily. Plenty of "custom" software is rented in disguise. The code sits in the developer's account, the hosting runs on their server, and the contract says you have a license to use it for as long as you keep paying. That arrangement can be fine for some businesses. It's a problem when nobody told you.

Ownership has layers

Ask "do I own it?" and you'll usually get a yes. Ask about each layer and the answers get more interesting:

  • The code. Who holds the intellectual property, and can you get a full copy of the source?
  • The data. Customer records, sales history, and settings. Can you export all of it, in a format another system can read?
  • The accounts. Hosting, domain, DNS, email delivery, text messaging, and payment processors. Whose name are they under?
  • The knowledge. Is there documentation good enough that a different developer could pick this up?

Eight questions to ask before you sign

1. Who owns the code once I've paid?

The contract should say it in plain words: the code is transferred to you, or you receive full ownership of what was built for you. "Licensed for your use" is a different thing, and it's worth knowing which one you're getting.

2. Where does the source code live, and when do I get access?

Best case, it lives in a repository under your own account from day one. Acceptable, it's transferred at launch. A warning sign is "we'll provide it if you ever need it."

3. Whose name are the hosting, domain, and service accounts under?

If the domain is registered to the agency, they control where your website points. If hosting is on their account, they control whether it stays up. These should be yours, with the developer added as a collaborator.

4. Can I export all of my data?

Ask for a specific format, such as CSV files or a database backup, and ask how the data is structured. If getting your own records out needs a custom project, you are renting the data too.

5. What would a new developer need to run this?

Setup steps, configuration, how the system is deployed, how backups work, and where the passwords and keys are kept. If the only person who knows is the one you're hiring, you have a single point of failure.

6. Does it depend on anything I'd have to keep paying for?

Third-party services are normal: email delivery, text messaging, payment processing. They should be listed, with their rough cost, and in your name. Watch for proprietary frameworks or licensed components that only the developer can use.

7. What does support cost afterward, and is it optional?

Support after launch is reasonable and often worth paying for. The question is whether the software still belongs to you if you decide not to. Good arrangements let you choose: run it yourself, pay for ongoing care, or hire someone else.

8. What does the handoff look like?

You want a checklist at the end: source code transferred, accounts confirmed, documentation delivered, your team trained. A project without a handoff is not finished, even if the software works.

Owning it doesn't mean running it yourself

This is the part people get backwards. Ownership is about control, not workload. You can own your code and data and still pay someone to host it, patch it, and answer the phone when something breaks. The difference is that you can leave. If the relationship stops working, you take everything with you.

That's also why monthly care plans aren't the same as a subscription trap. A plan that you can cancel without losing your software is a service. A plan that you can't cancel without losing your software is a lease.
